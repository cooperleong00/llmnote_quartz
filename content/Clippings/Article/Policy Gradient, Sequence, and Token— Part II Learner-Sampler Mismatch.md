Part II discusses the impact of the learner-sampler gap on policy gradient methods and how to adapt classical RL results accordingly. The discussion and notation follow [Part I](https://fengyao.notion.site/28b721e3f6c480b88b5be1d89512ac3a?pvs=25).

The learner-sampler mismatch is distinct from conventional off-policy RL because it arises from system differences rather than parameter differences. This discrepancy means some classical RL results cannot be applied directly.

## Adaptation of Trust Region

The core of the problem is that standard policy gradient objectives assume that data is sampled from the same policy as used in learning objective. When it's not, we run into issues.

In the single-turn sequence-level formulation, learner-sampler mismatch can be ”fixed“ by conducting sequence-level importance sampling.

In following discussions, we focus on the token-level or the multi-turn sequence-level objective, which more closely resembles Markov Decision Process (MDP).

### Ideal/Conventional Trust Region Objective

The ideal/conventional objective, which forms the basis for algorithms like TRPO and PPO, uses samples generated by $\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}$ to update $\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta}$.

$$
\begin{align}\small
\mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}},~a \sim \pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}(a|s)} \left[ \frac{\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}(a|s)} A^{\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}(s,a) \right],\;\text{s.t.} \; D_{KL}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm old}}\|\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta}) \leq C. \end{align}
$$

As discussed in [Part I](https://fengyao.notion.site/28b721e3f6c480b88b5be1d89512ac3a?pvs=25), in the conventional RL setting (i.e., no learner-sampler mismatch), this objective is not equivalent to $J(\theta)$, but instead is a lower bound for $J(\theta) - J(\theta_{\rm old})$.

### Naive Adoption in Modern RL

Using a specialized backend usually has the potential to accelerate rollout generation greatly. A naive approach might be to use data from the sampler policy, while gradient computation is still carried out via the learner policy.

$$
\begin{align}\small
\mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}},\;a \sim \textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}(a|s)} \left[ \frac{\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}(a|s)} A^{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}}(s,a) \right],\;\text{s.t.} \; D_{KL}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm old}}\|\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta}) \leq C \end{align}
$$

Similar to [the bandit formulation](https://fengyao.notion.site/28b721e3f6c480b88b5be1d89512ac3a?pvs=25#28b721e3f6c48103b2f3f08850dba1dd), the ratio $\frac{\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}}{\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}$ does not correctly re-weight samples drawn from $\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}$, and can be fixed via importance sampling. Moreover, different from the bandit formulation, the learner-sampler mismatch creates two additional disparities:

Visitation Frequency: $d^{\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}$ and $d^{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}}$ are different

Advantage: $A^{\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}(s,a)$ and $A^{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}}(s,a)$ are different.

In certain cases when estimation for $A^{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}}(s,a)$ can be used to estimate $A^{\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}(s,a)$ unbiasedly, the two disparities here can be fixed via a term similar to trajectory-level importance ratio. In general, these two disparities cannot be corrected by importance sampling, making existing theoretical analyses void.

That said, this naive adoption works well when the mismatch is small, and we will discuss a bit [later](https://fengyao.notion.site/28b721e3f6c480f8a4b0e1f8301d90ac?pvs=25#28b721e3f6c4812abd0bd2bf44345a56).

💡

Key Takeaway: Importance sampling can neutralize learner-sampler mismatch in the single-turn sequence-level formulation but not in the token-level or multi-turn sequence-level formulation.

### Adapting Trust Region

Our blog observes it yields good performance by using a corrected importance sampling ratio while maintaining the trust region centered on the previous learner policy. The corresponding objective is:

$$
\begin{align}\small
\mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}},a \sim \textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}(a|s)} \left[ \frac{\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}(a|s)}{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}(a|s)} A^{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}}(s,a) \right],\text{s.t.}\, D_{KL}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm old}}\|{\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta}}) \leq C \end{align}
$$

This objective works well in practice, to address [the mismatch](https://fengyao.notion.site/237721e3f6c48094ad67dad3ac091c56?pvs=25) or [off-policy rl](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00641). Here, we adapt the [Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.05477#page=3) of the TRPO paper as its theoretical justification.

Theorem 1 (connection between $J(\theta)$ and $L(\theta)$ under the Learner-Sampler Mismatch).

The adaptation is simple—using the triangle inequality for total variation distance, we can show that the performance improvement guarantee still largely holds. Specifically, $D_{TV}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm old}}, \textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}})$ is a constant to $\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta}$, and:

$$
\begin{align}
D_{TV}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}, \textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}) &\leq D_{TV}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}, {\pi^{\rm learner}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}) + D_{TV}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm old}}, \textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}) 
\end{align}
$$

While our surrogate objective is noisy, it remains a principled and empirically effective method for improving the true learner policy.

💡

Key Takeaway: Although the importance sampling cannot offset learner-sampler mismatch completely, the adapted surrogate function is a lower bound on the policy improvements, thus still serving as a reasonable learning objective.

### PPO-IS

In our [TIS blog](https://fengyao.notion.site/off-policy-rl), we also analyzed a variant called PPO-IS that attempts a more direct correction. It adjusts the importance ratio and the KL-divergence constraint to be directly between the new learner and the old sampler and can be written as follows,

$$
\begin{align}\small
\mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}},\;a \sim \textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}(a|s)} \left[ \frac{\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}(a|s)}{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}(a|s)} A^{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}}(s,a) \right],\;\text{s.t.} \; D_{KL}(\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}\|\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}) \leq C \end{align}
$$

This objective is theoretically valid; it is a lower bound on the learner's performance improvement in the sampler's environment. However, it performs [poorly in practice](https://fengyao.notion.site/237721e3f6c48094ad67dad3ac091c56?pvs=25#257721e3f6c480f2834afb7f4787b9f6). We believe the core issue lies in the constraint. Particularly, $D_{TV}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}, \textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}})$ measures the compound effect of $D_{TV}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm old}}, \textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}})$ and $D_{TV}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}, {\pi^{\rm learner}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}})$, where $D_{TV}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm old}}, \textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}})$ is a constant with regard to $\theta$. Correspondingly, the constant $C$ in Equation 5 should adapt to different instances based on $D_{TV}(\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm old}}, \textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}})$ .

## Mismatch from the FP32 Optimizer Perspective

Another way to understand the learner-sampler mismatch is through the lens of precision.

### Mixed-precision Training

Often, the optimization is typically conducted in FP32, while the gradient estimation (forward & backward) is conducted in a lower precision like BF16.

This leads to a subtle but important dynamic. We can think of the FP32 weights held by the optimizer as defining a "accurate policy." This policy was never executed in training. Instead, we work with its BF16 approximations.

If $\nabla_{\theta_{\rm bf16}}$ is a good approximation to $\nabla_{\theta_{\rm fp32}}$, then it seems reasonable to assume both $\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm bf16}}$ and $\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm{sampler}}_{\theta_{\rm bf16}}}$ are good approximations to $\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm fp32}}$. If this holds true, [the naive adaptation](https://fengyao.notion.site/28b721e3f6c480f8a4b0e1f8301d90ac?pvs=25#28b721e3f6c48170b15beeb4be9d18ba) can be justified as $\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{\theta_{\rm old}}}$ and $\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}$ are two different approximations to $\pi^{\rm fp32}_{\theta_{\rm old}}$.

$$
\begin{align}\small
\mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}},a \sim \textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}(a|s)} \left[ \frac{\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi^{\rm learner}_{\theta_{\rm old}}(a|s)} A^{\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm sampler}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}}(s,a) \right] 
\approx \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{{\pi^{\rm \tiny fp32}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}},a \sim {\pi^{\rm fp32}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}(a|s)} \left[ \frac{\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi^{\rm fp32}_{\theta_{\rm old}}(a|s)} A^{{\pi^{\rm fp32}_{{\theta_{\rm old}}}}}(s,a) \right] 
\nonumber\end{align}
$$

In fact, the naive adaptation served as the standard practice in most open-source RL frameworks, usually referred to as “recompute”.

### Implication of Large Learner-Sampler Mismatch

As the RL scales to larger model, longer context, and more challenging contexts, we observe that $\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm bf16}}$ and $\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm{sampler}}_{\theta_{\rm bf16}}}$ could result in vastly different results that for some $a$, $\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm{sampler}}_{\theta_{\rm bf16}}} (a) > 0.999$ while ${\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm bf16}}} (a) < 1e^{-3}$.

Naively, when there is a larger learner-sampler mismatch, the assumption is voided that both $\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm bf16}}$ and $\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm{sampler}}_{\theta_{\rm bf16}}}$ were good approximations—we should view them as different policies and adopt some importance sampling fix.

💡

Key Takeaway: For a small learner-sampler mismatch, we can treat $\pi^{\rm{learner}}_{\theta_{\rm bf16}}$ and $\textcolor{red}{\pi^{\rm{sampler}}_{\theta_{\rm bf16}}}$ as good approximations of the FP32 “accurate policy” in optimizer states; for a large learner-sampler mismatch, they should be treated as different policies.

## Mismatch and Output Perturbation

### Mismatch from the Perturbation Perspective

In most cases, we expect truncation errors to result in different but similar results, as the relative truncation error between $\theta_{\rm bf16}$ and $\theta_{\rm fp32}$ should be smaller than $10^{-2}$.

Correspondingly, the learner-sampler mismatch can be viewed as a special case of the output perturbation. The output perturbation is known to play an important role in training stabilization, e.g., it has been used in designing [Transformer initialization method](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.08249). A model with high output perturbation can be considered "ill-conditioned" or unstable. Here, we hypothesis that, instances with a large learner-sampler mismatch might also have large output perturbation in response to perturbation from other sources.

### Empirical Observation on the Perturbations

To test our hypothesis, we measured both the learner-sampler mismatch and the output perturbation on the DAPO-32B model:

Learner-Sampler mismatch was as measured as the difference in output probabilities (per-token): $|p_{\rm learner} (\theta) - \textcolor{red}{p_{\rm sampler}} (\theta)|$. We used TP2 in sampler and SP8 in learner here.

Output Perturbation was as measured adding tiny random noise to the model weights and measuring the change $|p_{\rm learner} (\theta+\delta) - p_{\rm learner} (\theta)|$, where $\delta_i$ is i.i.d. sampled from $\{1e^{-6}, -1 e^{-6}\}$ uniformly. We didn’t use TP and SP here.

For each generated sequence, we computed the average and maximum. As visualized in Figure B:

A strong correlation between the average learner-sampler mismatch and the average output perturbation. This supports our hypothesis that instances prone to the mismatch are also generally unstable.

Figure B. Output perturbation and mismatch of the first 512 samples from the DAPO training set. Left: Correlation between average learner-sampler mismatch and average output perturbation. Right: Correlation between the max learner-sampler mismatch and the max output perturbation.

💡

Key Takeaway: Average output perturbation has a strong correlation to the average learner-sampler mismatch, while max output perturbation has a weak correlation to the max learner-sampler mismatch.

#### Implication

As we verified empirically that training instances with a larger learner-sampler gap also has a large condition number, it is reasonable to filter-out those examples to avoid computing gradients over those ill-conditioned samples.

This observation aligns with [recent](https://ringtech.notion.site/icepop) [findings](https://yingru.notion.site/When-Speed-Kills-Stability-Demystifying-RL-Collapse-from-the-Training-Inference-Mismatch-271211a558b7808d8b12d403fd15edda) from the community, i.e., for samples with very large mismatch, it helps to stabilize training by masking those examples directly. From the original importance sampling perspective, this does not make sense—masking out those examples break the assumption of importance sampling and introduces additional bias. However, from the output perturbation perspective, this practice is well justified. It stabilizes the training by filtering out instances with a large condition number.

💡

Key Takeaway: Filtering out instances with a large mismatch helps to stabilize training, as large mismatch correlates with large output perturbation.

## Summary

As Part I gives a quick run-through on the connection of token-level loss and sequence-level loss, Part II analyses their relationship under existence of the learner-sampler mismatch. Particularly,

The Theorem 1 of the TRPO paper can be adapted to the learner-sampler mismatch case;

The learner-sampler mismatch can be viewed as a form of output perturbation. Our experiments show a strong correlation between the average mismatch and average output perturbation, suggesting that samples with a large mismatch to be ill-conditioned.
